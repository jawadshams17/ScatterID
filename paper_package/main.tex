\title{ScatterID 2.0: Quantum-Resilient W3C Verifiable Credentials with Minimally-Anchored Blockchain Storage and Zero-Knowledge Data Minimization}

\author[KFUEIT]{Jawad Shams\corref{cor1}}

\begin{abstract}
Digital credential infrastructures relying on classical public-key cryptography (e.g., Ed25519, ECDSA P-256, RSA-2048) face an existential threat from quantum computing algorithms (Shor's and Grover's). Because verifiable credentials (VCs) carry validity periods of 5 to 10 years, credentials issued today are vulnerable to ``Store-Now-Decrypt-Later'' (SNDL) attacks. In this paper, we present ScatterID 2.0, a production-ready, open-source framework that combines NIST FIPS 204 (ML-DSA-65) post-quantum signatures, client-side RFC 8785 JSON Canonicalization Scheme (JCS) with 16-byte CSPRNG salting, and permissioned Hyperledger Fabric blockchain storage. Raw identity attributes never leave the user's client device; instead, only a one-way SHA3-256 hash commitment (\texttt{dataHash}) and state proofs (\texttt{AnchorProof} / \texttt{QueryProof}) are anchored on-chain. To eliminate ledger bloat caused by large lattice signatures ($\approx 3.3$~KB per credential), ScatterID 2.0 adopts an off-chain signature storage model, reducing on-chain storage overhead by over 99\%. We evaluate 15 cryptographic primitives ($n = 10{,}000$ iterations, 1,000 warm-up) across standard, 50\%, and 20\% selective disclosure patterns. Empirical benchmarks on a development workstation (AMD Ryzen 5 3600, 16\,GiB RAM, Windows 11) demonstrate that ML-DSA-44 achieves a verification latency of 31.9~$\mu$s, outperforming classical Ed25519 (34.8~$\mu$s) by 8.3\%, while expanding credential size from 800~bytes to 3,947~bytes. We formalize ScatterID's tiered key hierarchy, zero-trust mTLS service topology, background ledger reconciliation daemon, and zero-dependency cross-language offline verification engine (Node.js and Python) achieving 100\% output parity. ScatterID 2.0 establishes a concrete, quantum-resistant reference architecture for privacy-preserving digital identity systems. Raw benchmark data are publicly available at \texttt{benchmarks/results/} \cite{shams2026benchmarks}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Digital identity management stands at a critical juncture in contemporary cybersecurity \cite{allen2016path, krul2024sok}. Centralized identity infrastructures maintained by monolithic enterprise identity providers create dangerous single points of failure, exposing massive repositories of personal data to breaches, unauthorized profiling, and systemic outage vulnerabilities \cite{malik2023developing, ormeir2019dynamic}. While Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI) frameworks and the W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model 2.0 \cite{w3cvc2024} have introduced decentralized, user-centric cryptographic trust mechanisms, the foundational mathematical primitives underpinning these architectures remain acutely vulnerable to quantum computing cryptanalysis \cite{shor1997polynomial, grover1996fast}.

Current digital identity deployments heavily depend on elliptic curve signatures (e.g., Ed25519, ECDSA secp256k1, NIST P-256) and legacy RSA primitives \cite{w3cvc2024}. However, the emergence of fault-tolerant quantum hardware running Shor's algorithm \cite{shor1997polynomial} threatens to solve the discrete logarithm problem and prime factorization in polynomial time, completely destroying the security properties of classical public-key cryptography.

\subsection{Motivation \& Quantum Threat Window}
The urgency of transitioning digital credentials to Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) is governed by the ``Store-Now-Decrypt-Later'' (SNDL) attack paradigm \cite{mosca2018cybersecurity}. Adversaries are actively capturing and archiving encrypted communications and signed credential payloads today. Physical identity assets---such as passports and academic degrees---typically possess 5 to 10-year validity windows. Consequently, digital credentials signed using Ed25519 or ECDSA P-256 today will remain operationally active during the projected advent of quantum advantage \cite{ibm2023quantum, nistir8547}.

NIST IR 8547 \cite{nistir8547} specifies that 112-bit security primitives (e.g., RSA-2048, P-224) are formally deprecated, while 128-bit primitives (e.g., P-256) will be disallowed post-2035. Deploying quantum-resistant identity infrastructure is therefore a pressing requirement for operational digital sovereignty.

\subsection{Core Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}
This work evaluates the practical feasibility, architectural engineering, and performance trade-offs of post-quantum verifiable credentials through ScatterID 2.0:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{RQ1 (Cryptographic Performance):} How do NIST FIPS 204 ML-DSA lattice signatures compare against classical primitives in issuance latency, verification throughput, and payload footprint?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (ZK Data Minimization Overhead):} What is the computational overhead of enforcing client-side RFC 8785 JCS \cite{rfc8785} canonicalization, 16-byte CSPRNG salting, and SHA3-256 pre-image commitments?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (Blockchain Storage Efficiency):} How does minimally-anchored Hyperledger Fabric Raft storage impact throughput and revocation response times?
\item \textbf{RQ4 (Cross-Language Offline Verification Parity):} Can zero-dependency standalone verifiers in Node.js and Python maintain 100\% mathematical output parity with comparable runtime performance?
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\subsection{W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model 2.0 \& Selective Disclosure}
The W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model 2.0 \cite{w3cvc2024} defines a standardized role triangle of Issuer, Holder, and Verifier, backed by a Verifiable Data Registry (VDR). Selective disclosure mechanisms enable holders to reveal only a subset of attributes. In classical systems, this is implemented via Selective Disclosure JWT (SD-JWT) \cite{ietfsdjwt2024} or pairing-based zero-knowledge proofs (BBS+ signatures). However, BBS+ relies on bilinear pairings over elliptic curves, which are fundamentally broken under Shor's algorithm \cite{shor1997polynomial}.

\subsection{Post-Quantum Cryptographic Schemes}
NIST standardized three primary post-quantum digital signature algorithms in August 2024 \cite{fips204, fips203, fips205}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{ML-DSA (FIPS 204):} Derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium \cite{ducas2018crystals}, relying on Module Learning With Errors (M-LWE) and Module Short Integer Solution (M-SIS). Parameter sets: ML-DSA-44 (Category 2), ML-DSA-65 (Category 3), ML-DSA-87 (Category 5).
\item \textbf{Falcon (FIPS 206 Draft):} Built on NTRU lattices \cite{fouque2020falcon}. Compact signatures (651~B for Falcon-512) but requires double-precision floating-point arithmetic.
\item \textbf{SLH-DSA (FIPS 205):} Hash-based \cite{bernstein2019sphincs}. Conservative security, but large signature sizes (7.8--49.8~KB) and slow signing (15--542~ms).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Client-Side Data Minimization \& Commitment Protocol}
ScatterID 2.0 enforces absolute zero-knowledge data minimization at the client boundary. Raw identity claims never cross network boundaries or touch backend storage.

Let $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k\}$ represent a set of claim key-value pairs. The issuance workflow follows a three-step protocol:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Canonicalization:} The client SDK applies RFC 8785 JCS \cite{rfc8785} to establish a deterministic representation:
\begin{equation}
\text{claimCanonical} = \text{RFC8785\_JCS}(\mathcal{C})
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Cryptographic Salting:} A 16-byte (128-bit) CSPRNG salt ($s \leftarrow \text{CSPRNG}(16)$) prevents brute-force dictionary attacks under Grover's algorithm \cite{grover1996fast}:
\begin{equation}
\text{saltHex} = \text{HexEncode}(s)
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Pre-Image Hash Commitment:} A one-way SHA3-256 digest over the concatenated salt and canonical claim string:
\begin{equation}
\text{dataHash} = \text{SHA3-256}(s \parallel \text{claimCanonical})
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Only the resulting 256-bit \texttt{dataHash} is transmitted to the Verification Gateway API (\texttt{POST /issue}). The raw attributes $c_i$ and salt $s$ remain exclusively on the user's local device.

\subsection{PQC Cryptographic Engine \& Vault KMS Integration}
The core signing infrastructure sits within an isolated, mTLS-protected microservice (\texttt{components/crypto}). The signing engine leverages Python \texttt{liboqs-python} bindings \cite{liboqs2024} for NIST FIPS 204 ML-DSA-65 (CRYSTALS-Dilithium3). Key material is managed by HashiCorp Vault KMS utilizing AppRole authentication:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{pqc}} = \text{ML-DSA-65.Sign}(\text{SK}_{\text{issuer}}, \text{dataHash})
\end{equation}
Upon completion, volatile memory key buffers undergo explicit zeroization (\texttt{zeroize()}) to prevent side-channel memory inspection \cite{shams2026security}.

\subsection{Minimally-Anchored Hyperledger Fabric Network \& Off-Chain Signature Storage}
To prevent ledger bloat, ScatterID 2.0 decouples signature storage from consensus state. The Hyperledger Fabric permissioned chaincode anchors only four immutable state attributes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{state}} = \{\text{dataHash}, \text{publicKeyId}, \text{timestamp}, \text{revoked}\}
\end{equation}
The post-quantum signature ($\sigma_{\text{pqc}}$) remains strictly off-chain within the W3C VC payload, reducing on-chain storage from 3.3~KB to 32~bytes ($>99\%$ reduction). The chaincode enforces:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \texttt{AnchorProof(dataHash, publicKeyId)}: Validates caller authorization and records the 32-byte state anchor onto the Raft consensus ledger.
\item \texttt{QueryProof(dataHash)}: Retrieves revocation state and issuance timestamp from the world state database.
\item \texttt{RevokeProof(dataHash)}: Administrative revocation transaction, restricted to \texttt{REVOKE\_API\_KEY} scope.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tiered Key Security \& Multi-Zone Network Topology}
ScatterID separates operational capabilities into four non-overlapping authorization tiers \cite{shams2026security}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \texttt{VERIFICATION\_API\_KEY}: Scoped for credential issuance (\texttt{/issue}), status queries, and audit log inspection.
\item \texttt{REVOKE\_API\_KEY}: Scoped strictly for administrative revocation actions (\texttt{POST /revoke}).
\item \texttt{GATEWAY\_API\_KEY}: Authenticates local operator dashboard proxy requests using constant-time \texttt{crypto.timingSafeEqual} comparisons \cite{nodejs_timingsafe, kocher1996timing}.
\item \texttt{CRYPTO\_SERVICE\_API\_KEY}: Internal mTLS bearer secret guarding the post-quantum signing engine on port 5001.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Standalone Offline Cross-Language Verification Protocol}
To support air-gapped auditing, ScatterID 2.0 provides dual standalone CLI tools in Node.js (\texttt{tools/verify\_offline.js}) and Python (\texttt{tools/verify\_offline.py}). Verification follows a two-level hierarchy:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Level 1 (Pre-Image Commitment Check):} Re-computes $\text{dataHash}' = \text{SHA3-256}(s \parallel \text{RFC8785\_JCS}(\mathcal{C}))$ and asserts:
\begin{equation}
\text{dataHash}' \stackrel{?}{=} \text{dataHash}_{\text{claimed}}
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Level 2 (Post-Quantum Signature Check):} Executes ML-DSA-65 verification against the issuer's public key:
\begin{equation}
\text{ML-DSA-65.Verify}(\text{PK}_{\text{issuer}}, \text{dataHash}, \sigma_{\text{pqc}}) \stackrel{?}{=} \text{TRUE}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Primitive Cryptographic Benchmarks (RQ1)}
\label{sec:rq1}
We evaluated 15 cryptographic algorithms (4 classical, 11 post-quantum) on a development workstation (AMD Ryzen 5 3600 @ 3.6~GHz, 16~GiB DDR4, Windows~11 Pro 23H2). Each primitive underwent 1,000 warm-up iterations (discarded) and $n = 10{,}000$ benchmark iterations timed with \texttt{time.perf\_counter\_ns()}. SPHINCS+ variants were evaluated at $n = 1{,}000$ due to extreme signing latency. Raw per-iteration CSV and summary JSON (mean, median, SEM, CV) are committed at \texttt{benchmarks/results/} \cite{shams2026benchmarks}. Table~\ref{tab:primitives} and Figure~\ref{fig:benchmarks} detail results.

\textbf{Key Finding (RQ1 -- Verification Speedup):} ML-DSA-44 achieves a verification latency of 31.9~$\mu$s (33,003~ops/s), outperforming classical Ed25519 (34.8~$\mu$s, 29,070~ops/s) by 8.3\%. This speedup arises because ML-DSA verification relies primarily on SIMD-vectorized Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) matrix-vector polynomial multiplications in C (\texttt{liboqs}), whereas Ed25519 requires computationally intensive scalar-field point multiplications over Curve25519. However, ML-DSA-44 expands the VC payload footprint from 800~bytes to 3,947~bytes ($\approx 4.9\times$).

\subsection{Client-Side JCS + SHA3-256 Overhead (RQ2)}
\label{sec:rq2}
To isolate the client-side data minimization overhead independently from signing latency (RQ2), we benchmarked the composite operation consisting of: (1) RFC 8785 JSON Canonicalization Scheme (JCS) applied to a 10-field credential claim set, (2) generation of a 16-byte CSPRNG salt, and (3) SHA3-256 hash computation over the concatenated salt and canonical claim string. Over $n = 10{,}000$ iterations, the composite JCS + salting + SHA3-256 operation completed in a mean of \textbf{4.2~$\mu$s} (median: 3.8~$\mu$s, SEM: 0.03~$\mu$s, CV: 7.1\%). This represents approximately 5.2\% of the total ML-DSA-44 issuance latency (80.1~$\mu$s), confirming that the zero-knowledge data minimization protocol imposes negligible client-side computational overhead.

\section{Security Analysis \& Operational Discussion}
\label{sec:security}

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threatmodel}
Figure~\ref{fig:threatmodel} illustrates ScatterID 2.0's threat model. We assume the following attacker capabilities:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Quantum Adversary:} A fault-tolerant quantum computer capable of running Shor's algorithm against classical elliptic curve discrete logarithm problems, and Grover's algorithm against symmetric primitives with $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})$ speedup. This adversary may have harvested SNDL ciphertext archives.
\item \textbf{Network Adversary:} A man-in-the-middle attacker capable of intercepting, replaying, or injecting messages on any unencrypted channel.
\item \textbf{Malicious Verifier:} A credential verifier who attempts to extract raw claim attributes from the credential or derive the salt value from an observed \texttt{dataHash}.
\item \textbf{Compromised Vault Instance:} An attacker with read access to Vault storage who attempts to recover key material.
\item \textbf{Credential Forger:} An attacker who attempts to construct a valid ML-DSA-65 signature without knowledge of the issuer secret key.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Security Guarantees}
ScatterID 2.0 provides the following provable security guarantees against the threat model above:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Signature Security:} ML-DSA-65 provides NIST Security Category~3 ($>128$-bit quantum security strength), resisting quantum lattice attacks (e.g., BKZ lattice reduction).
\item \textbf{Pre-Image Commitment Security:} The combination of 16-byte (128-bit) CSPRNG salting and SHA3-256 requires $2^{128}$ quantum operations under Grover's algorithm ($\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})$), providing full resistance against preimage recovery by malicious verifiers.
\item \textbf{Zero-Trust Key Isolation:} Global TLS bypasses are entirely removed. Internal microservice communication requires mTLS validated against ScatterID's internal Certificate Authority. Gateway authorization tokens are compared using constant-time \texttt{crypto.timingSafeEqual} comparisons \cite{nodejs_timingsafe, kocher1996timing} to eliminate timing side-channel exposures \cite{shams2026security}.
\item \textbf{Vault Compromise Mitigation:} AppRole authentication, memory zeroization after key use, and Vault transit engine encryption ensure that a read-only compromise of Vault storage does not expose plaintext key material.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations \& Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
We acknowledge the following limitations and threats to validity:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Single-Node Fabric Consortium:} RQ3 measurements were collected on a single-node local Raft consortium without multi-organization adversarial testing. Production deployments with multi-peer endorsement policies will exhibit different throughput characteristics due to additional endorsement round-trips and network latency between organizations.
\item \textbf{Benchmark Hardware Scope:} All RQ1 measurements were obtained on a single development workstation (AMD Ryzen 5 3600, 16~GiB RAM, Windows~11). Results may differ on server-grade hardware (e.g., Intel Xeon with AVX-512) or embedded/constrained devices. The benchmark script (\texttt{benchmarks/run\_crypto\_bench.py}) supports CPU affinity pinning on Linux bare-metal for improved reproducibility.
\item \textbf{liboqs Implementation-Specific Timings:} Benchmarks use the Open Quantum Safe \texttt{liboqs} reference implementation (v0.11.0) \cite{liboqs2024}. Timings reflect reference C implementations, not constant-time-audited or hardware-accelerated production builds. Vendor-specific optimizations (e.g., Intel QS library) may yield lower latencies.
\item \textbf{SD-JWT as Comparison Baseline:} SD-JWT selective disclosure is used as a comparison baseline for payload size analysis. ScatterID 2.0's native format is W3C \texttt{DataIntegrityProof}, not SD-JWT. The SD-JWT figures in Table~\ref{tab:sdjwt} represent approximate overhead projections for cross-format comparison only.
\item \textbf{Independent Implementation Status:} ScatterID 2.0 is an open-source, independently developed research prototype without peer-reviewed third-party security audits. While the implementation follows production-oriented practices (key zeroization, mTLS, constant-time comparisons), independent cryptographic engineering review is recommended before production deployment in critical identity infrastructure.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
In this paper, we presented ScatterID 2.0, a quantum-resilient W3C Verifiable Credential infrastructure combining NIST FIPS 204 ML-DSA-65 signatures, client-side RFC 8785 JCS \cite{rfc8785} data minimization, and minimally-anchored Hyperledger Fabric blockchain storage. By anchoring only a 32-byte cryptographic commitment (\texttt{dataHash}) while storing signatures off-chain, ScatterID 2.0 reduces on-chain storage bloat by over 99\%. Our empirical evaluation of 15 cryptographic schemes ($n = 10{,}000$) confirms that ML-DSA-44 achieves a verification latency of 31.9~$\mu$s, outperforming classical Ed25519 by 8.3\% while expanding credential size to 3,947~bytes. Client-side JCS + SHA3-256 data minimization overhead is 4.2~$\mu$s (5.2\% of total issuance latency), confirming negligible privacy overhead. Fabric \texttt{QueryProof} reads achieve 42.7~req/s, while cross-language offline verification maintains 100\% parity with Node.js running $1.34\times$ faster at shell-invocation level. Raw benchmark data traceable to every table value are publicly available at \texttt{benchmarks/results/} \cite{shams2026benchmarks}. ScatterID 2.0 demonstrates that zero-knowledge post-quantum identity verification is practically deployable today, providing a robust reference architecture for digital trust in the quantum threat era.
